Abstract

Pollination networks sustain ~88% of flowering plants and 75% of global food crops. We analyse 172 real-world bipartite networks across 6 continents using cascading co-extinction simulation, climate stress modelling, adaptive rewiring, early warning signal detection, Random Forest extinction risk prediction, GraphSAGE-based role discovery, and species transplantation to characterise structural vulnerability and collapse dynamics at continent scale.

1

Dataset & Network Model

Bipartite graph G = (U ∪ V, E): pollinators (U), plants (V). 172 city-level datasets from Web of Life were grouped into 6 continental meta-networks for macro-scale comparison, while all 172 were retained for network-level GNN analysis.

  • Degree Centrality — generalist identification
  • Betweenness Centrality — bridge species
  • Eigenvector Centrality — systemic influence
  • NODF Nestedness — specialist–generalist hierarchy
  • Modularity Q — community compartmentalisation

4

Cascading Co-extinction

Pollinators removed sequentially; plants co-extinct when all links lost. 50 repeats for random removal average.

2.5–4× difference in network half-life. North America (Q=0.270) collapses earliest — targeted removal generates 80–300 isolated components.
5

Climate Stress Simulation

Climate stress modelled as gradual reduction in interaction strength (s: 0→1). All networks collapse rapidly beyond s ≈ 0.75–0.85.

Pollinators decline before plants across all networks — monitoring pollinator abundance provides earlier ecosystem stress warning than monitoring plant abundance.
6

Early Warning Signal Detection

Critical Slowing Down (CSD): rising variance and lag-1 autocorrelation precede collapse bifurcation. CSD autocorrelation threshold = 0.9.

  • Variance rises at stress ≈ 0.70–0.75, before critical zone at ~0.79
  • Warning window: 10–30 years before catastrophic transition
  • Oceania shows earliest warning — limited island redundancy
7

Adaptive Rewiring (p = 0.6)

Surviving plants seek alternative pollinators proportional to degree when a partner is lost. Resilience gain = area between rewiring and no-rewiring curves.

  • Largest gain: Europe & Oceania (high nestedness)
  • Smallest: Asia & N. America (modular, locally saturated)
  • Benefit positively correlated with nestedness
Conservation: Habitat corridors enabling pollinators to encounter novel plants significantly boost resilience beyond species-level protection.
8

ML Extinction Risk Prediction

Random Forest classifier (100 trees, 5-fold CV) on structural node features. Consistent feature importance across all 6 continental networks.

Eigenvector dominates (~40%). Conserving pollinators by interaction frequency alone misses systemically critical species at strategic positions.

◆   Key Findings & Conclusions   ◆
01
Continental Architecture Diversity
South America (Q=0.773) most stress-resistant. North America (Q=0.270) most vulnerable — 2.5–4× faster collapse under targeted vs. random removal. Africa's high nestedness (NODF=18.2) drives synchronous tipping-point collapse.
02
GNN Corpus & Eigenvector Dominance
Across 172 networks, Connector (38%) and Specialist (37%) are co-dominant regimes. Eigenvector centrality consistently outperforms raw degree as extinction risk predictor (~40% RF importance), arguing for eigenvector-based conservation prioritisation.
03
Early Warning & Rewiring Buffer
CSD signals detectable 10–30 years before catastrophic collapse. Adaptive rewiring improves resilience across all networks — highest in nested communities. Managed transplantation modestly improves resilience when species bridges structural gaps.
